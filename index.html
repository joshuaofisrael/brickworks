A Short History of Bricks

People have been shaping mud into building blocks for roughly nine thousand years. The earliest known bricks, found in the Middle East, were simply sun-dried lumps of clay mixed with straw, pressed by hand and left to bake in the heat.

The big leap came with firing. Once builders discovered that clay heated in a kiln turned hard and water-resistant, bricks could survive rain and frost. Mesopotamian cities, the Indus Valley settlements and later the Romans all relied on fired brick, and Roman legions carried mobile kilns across their empire.

After a quieter period in parts of Europe, brick returned strongly in the Middle Ages, especially in regions short of good building stone such as the Low Countries and the Baltic coast. The Industrial Revolution brought steam-powered presses and continuous kilns, making bricks cheap enough to build whole cities of terraced houses, mills and railway viaducts.

Types & Sizes

Common brick

Everyday structural brick, usually hidden behind render or used in internal walls.

Facing brick

Made for looks: consistent colour, texture and sharp edges for the visible face of a wall.

Engineering brick

Dense, strong and low in water absorption. Ideal for foundations, sewers and retaining walls.

Firebrick

Refractory brick that withstands extreme heat, used to line fireplaces, kilns and furnaces.

Handmade & stock

Thrown into sanded moulds, giving creases and soft edges that look great in older houses.

Perforated & frogged

Holes or a hollow "frog" reduce weight and help the mortar key in.

Bonds & Patterns

A "stretcher" shows its long side, a "header" shows its end. Bonds mix them so vertical joints never line up, spreading the load.

How Bricks Are Made

  1. Winning the clay. Clay and shale are dug from quarries and left to weather.
  2. Preparation. The raw material is crushed, screened and mixed with water to a workable consistency.
  3. Shaping. Clay is either extruded as a long column and wire-cut, pressed into moulds, or thrown by hand.
  4. Drying. Green bricks are dried slowly over a day or more so they don't crack in the kiln.
  5. Firing. Kilns heat bricks to around 900–1,200 °C. The temperature and clay minerals decide the final colour, from pale yellow to deep blue-black.
  6. Cooling & sorting. Bricks cool gradually, then are graded and packed.

Famous Brick Buildings

Malbork Castle, Poland

Often described as the largest brick castle in the world, built by the Teutonic Knights.

Hampton Court Palace, England

Tudor red-brick grandeur with spectacular decorative chimneys.

Great Mosque of Djenné, Mali

The world's largest mud-brick building, re-plastered by the community every year.

Chilehaus, Hamburg

A ship-like 1920s office block and a landmark of Brick Expressionism.

St Pancras, London

A Victorian Gothic showpiece of red brick at the station's front.

Ctesiphon arch, Iraq

An ancient arch that remains among the largest single-span brick vaults ever built.

Care & Repointing Tips

  • Mortar should be softer than the brick. Water and movement should affect the joint, which you can replace, not the brick. On old houses, use lime mortar instead of hard cement.
  • Rake out properly. Remove old mortar to about 2–2.5 times the joint height (typically 15–25 mm) without chipping brick edges.
  • Dampen first. Lightly wet the joints so the brick doesn't suck water out of fresh mortar.
  • Match the finish. Copy the original joint profile (flush, bucket-handle, weatherstruck) and the colour of the sand.
  • Protect while curing. Shield new work from frost, strong sun and heavy rain for several days.
  • Clean gently. Avoid sandblasting or high-pressure washing, which strip the fired skin. Use low pressure water and a soft brush.
  • Fix the cause. Damp patches usually come from leaking gutters or high ground levels, not the bricks themselves.
