How to repoint a brick wall properly

Plain-English mortar guidance for bricklayers and site work

Short answer

Rake out old mortar to 2 to 2½ times the joint width, which is usually ½ to 1 inch for brick, then refill in thin layers with a mortar no harder than the brick or the original mortar. That is the core method in the US National Park Service's Preservation Brief 2. Fix whatever caused the decay first, and match the original joint colour, texture and profile.

1. Find the cause before you repoint

The NPS brief warns it is wrong to assume repointing alone solves problems caused by something else. Leaking roofs or gutters, settlement, rising damp and extreme exposure should be dealt with first, or the new joints will fail the same way.

2. Choose a softer, matching mortar

The new mortar should be as permeable as, or more permeable than, and as soft as or softer than, the historic mortar, and softer than the brick. The idea is that the joint, which can be renewed, takes the stress and moisture instead of the brick. A hard portland cement joint on soft brick can crack and spall the brick edges.

The brief puts the ASTM types in order of approximate strength: Type M (2,500 psi), S (1,800 psi), N (750 psi), O (350 psi) and K (75 psi), and gives lime rich mixes such as Type O (1:2:9) and Type K (1:3:11) as examples for soft historic brick. Matching the sand is the key to matching colour and texture. More detail: lime vs cement mortar and mortar types N, S, M and O.

3. Rake out carefully

  • Depth: 2 to 2½ times the joint width, roughly ½ to 1 inch on most brick joints. Also remove any loose mortar beyond that depth.
  • Hand tools (plugging chisel and hammer) give the best result on soft or historic brick.
  • Power grinders used by unskilled hands chip the arrises and widen head joints. The NPS brief says power saws on thin brick joints almost always damage the brick. A skilled mason may use a thin diamond blade on wide horizontal joints of hard cement mortar.
  • Try a test panel first; about 3 ft by 3 ft is usually enough for brickwork.

4. Prepare and fill in layers

  1. Rinse the joints with water to remove dust. At filling time they should be damp with no standing water.
  2. Fill any extra deep spots first.
  3. Pack about ¼ inch of mortar into the back of the joint. When it is thumbprint hard, add the next ¼ inch layer.
  4. Repeat until the joint is filled flush, then tool to match the original profile.

The brief also says to keep water to the minimum for repointing mortar, use it within about 30 minutes to 1½ hours once portland cement is added, and not retemper it. That is stricter than normal new build practice, explained in how long mortar stays usable.

5. Cure and clean

Protect the new pointing from fast drying, hard frost and driving rain while it cures; lime rich mortars need longer. Clean mortar smears gently, never by sandblasting. See cleaning brickwork.

How to repoint brick FAQ

How deep should I rake out mortar for repointing?

To 2 to 2½ times the width of the joint, which is about ½ to 1 inch for most brick joints, according to NPS Preservation Brief 2.

Is repointing the same as tuckpointing?

Not strictly. Repointing means replacing deteriorated mortar. Tuckpointing technically describes a decorative raised joint or lime putty joint applied over flush joints, though the terms are often mixed up.

Can I use cement mortar to repoint an old brick house?

Use a mortar no harder than the original and softer than the brick. On soft historic brick that usually means lime rich mixes. A hard cement joint can damage the brick.

Should I use an angle grinder to remove mortar?

Hand tools are safest. The NPS brief says power saws on thin brick joints almost always damage the brick; a skilled mason may use a thin diamond blade on wide horizontal joints of hard cement mortar.

How thick should each layer be?

About ¼ inch, letting each layer reach thumbprint hardness before adding the next.
