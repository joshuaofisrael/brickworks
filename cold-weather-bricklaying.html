Cold weather bricklaying: what changes below 40 °F (4.4 °C)

Short answer

In US practice, cold weather rules start when the air is below 40 °F (4.4 °C). From there the TMS Specification, as summarised in BIA Technical Note 1, asks you to heat the mixing water or sand so mortar is between 40 °F and 120 °F (4.4 °C to 48.9 °C), never lay frozen or iced units, and cover new work for at least 24 hours. Each colder band adds more heat and more protection.

Temperature bands and what each one requires

Preparation and construction rules are based on the air temperature while you work. Protection rules after the wall is built are based on the mean daily temperature. The table condenses the TMS Specification requirements reproduced in BIA Technical Note 1.

Do not heat water or aggregate above 140 °F (60 °C).Cover new masonry with a weather resistive membrane for 24 hours.
32 °F to 25 °F (0 °C to −3.9 °C)All of the above, and keep the mortar above freezing until it is used.As above.

Why cold matters to mortar

Mortar mixed with cold materials tends to carry less water, more air and less early strength, which BIA links to lower compressive strength, weaker bond and poorer resistance to water penetration. If fresh mortar freezes while it is still wet, the expanding ice damages it. BIA notes that mortar with a water content above about 6 percent is likely to be damaged by freezing.

The target is not hot mortar. BIA describes 60 °F to 80 °F (15.6 °C to 26.7 °C) as the ideal mortar temperature and warns that mortar above 120 °F can flash set, which lowers strength and bond.

Practical site habits in winter

  • Heat the water first. BIA notes water holds heat better than sand. If sand must be heated, do it slowly and never use scorched sand.
  • Mix smaller batches. Use mortar before it cools. A mixing time of about 3 minutes is suggested, because long mixing raises air content.
  • Keep the normal working window. Mortar should still be used within 2½ hours of initial mixing, the same as normal weather.
  • Store materials off the ground and covered. Bagged cement, lime and brick on raised platforms under tarps stay dry and free of snow and ice.
  • Cover the top of the wall. Protective sheets should be weighted down and run at least 2 ft (0.6 m) down each side.
  • Accelerators do not replace heat. Set accelerating admixtures may be used, but heating and protection are still required. Calcium chloride accelerators are a known source of chloride efflorescence, see our efflorescence guide.

Mix choice also shifts. BIA suggests stiffer, sand richer mixes within the permitted ranges and allows Type III cement for faster early strength. Ingredients that hold water, such as extra lime or very fine sand, are not recommended in cold weather. The mortar types guide covers the permitted ranges.

Cold weather bricklaying FAQ

At what temperature can you not lay bricks?

There is no single cut off. Under the TMS Specification cold weather procedures apply below 40 °F (4.4 °C), and work can continue in colder weather only with heated materials, windbreaks, heated enclosures below 20 °F and protection after laying. Units colder than 20 °F or with ice on them should not be laid.

How long should new brickwork be covered in cold weather?

At least 24 hours after completion when the mean daily temperature is 40 °F or below. In the colder bands the cover becomes insulating blankets or a heated enclosure, and grouted masonry needs 48 hours unless the grout uses only Type III cement.

Can I add antifreeze to mortar?

The guidance does not treat any admixture as a substitute for heating and protection. Set accelerators may be used but heating and protection still apply, and calcium chloride based products can cause chloride efflorescence.

What if new brickwork freezes?

BIA notes that frozen new masonry may be moistened after thawing to restart hydration so it can keep gaining strength. Badly damaged work, such as crumbling joints, should be assessed before it is loaded.

How hot should the mixing water be?

Hot enough that the mixed mortar is between 40 °F and 120 °F, but the water or sand itself should never be heated above 140 °F (60 °C).
