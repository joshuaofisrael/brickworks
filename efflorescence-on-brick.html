Short answer

Efflorescence is a white, powdery deposit of soluble salts left on the brick face when water evaporates. It needs three things at once: salts, water to dissolve them, and pores that carry the solution to the surface. It is normally harmless. On new walls it usually fades within about a year. Light deposits come off with a dry brush. Recurring efflorescence means water is getting in, and that should be fixed first.

Is it efflorescence, lime run or scum?

DepositWhat it looks likeKey test
EfflorescenceWhite, powdery, crystalline, often fluffyWater soluble: it dissolves and often vanishes after rain, then returns
Lime run (calcium carbonate)White or grey crusty streaks running down the wallNot water soluble once carbonated. Hard crust, not powder
White scumThin whitish filmOften linked to cleaning on dry brick, see our cleaning guide

BIA Technical Note 23 points out that lime run is not caused by the lime in mortar; hydrated lime can actually reduce the risk.

Where the salts come from

BIA Technical Note 23A lists the most common salts as sulfates and carbonates of sodium, potassium, calcium, magnesium and aluminium. They can come from mortar, grout, concrete, backing block, soil, groundwater, rain, misused chemical cleaners and nearby pollution. Brick gets the blame because it is darker, so the white shows, but BIA says it is much more common for the salts to come from cement based materials in contact with the brick.

Chloride efflorescence usually traces back to calcium chloride accelerators in mortar, sea water contamination of sand or units, or hydrochloric (muriatic) acid used to clean.

New building bloom

Efflorescence on brickwork less than a year old is called new building bloom. It tends to appear evenly across the wall as construction water dries out. BIA says it generally disappears with time if the wall can dry, and its cleaning note recommends allowing one year of weathering for it to clear naturally.

How to remove it

  1. Find and stop the water first. Leaking gutters, missing copings, open joints, failed flashing and soil against the wall are common causes. Cleaning without fixing the source just resets the clock.
  2. Let the wall dry.
  3. Dry brush. Light efflorescence comes off with a stiff fibre brush, dry or with a little water.
  4. Stubborn deposits: a proprietary cleaner used exactly to the maker's instructions, after a small trial patch. BIA advises against unbuffered muriatic or hydrofluoric acid.

Coatings are not recommended as the only treatment; BIA lists that explicitly.

Prevention on new work

  • Store brick and bagged materials off the ground and covered.
  • Cover unfinished walls at the end of each day.
  • Fill and tool joints properly so rain stays out, see mortar joint profiles.
  • Keep below grade brick waterproofed and separate brick from backing with an air space or damp proofing.
  • Avoid calcium chloride accelerators.

Efflorescence on brick FAQ

Is efflorescence harmful to brick?

Usually not. It is a cosmetic salt deposit. It is a sign that water is moving through the wall, and persistent wetting is what causes real damage, so recurring efflorescence is worth investigating.

Will efflorescence go away on its own?

On new brickwork it usually does. New building bloom typically clears within about a year of weathering if the wall can dry.

Should I use vinegar or muriatic acid on efflorescence?

Start with a dry stiff brush. BIA advises against unbuffered muriatic acid, which can itself leave chloride salts and stains. Use a proprietary cleaner to the maker's instructions only for stubborn deposits.

Why does efflorescence keep coming back?

Because water keeps getting into the masonry and dissolving salts. Fix the water source before cleaning again.

Does sealing brick stop efflorescence?

BIA does not recommend coatings as the only treatment. Trapping salts behind a coating can push the problem elsewhere.
