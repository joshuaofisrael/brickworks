How Far Apart Should Brick Expansion Joints Be?

Spacing, corners and sealant for bricklayers, with sources

Short answer

In brick veneer, BIA says to space vertical expansion joints no more than 25 ft apart in plain walls and no more than 20 ft apart in walls with multiple openings, put one at or near each corner, and put a horizontal joint under every shelf angle. The designer sets the exact locations on the drawings, so build them as drawn and keep mortar out of them.

1. Why brick walls need expansion joints

BIA Technical Note 18A explains that an expansion joint separates brickwork into segments, which reduces the cumulative effect of movement from temperature, moisture expansion, elastic deformation, settlement and creep. Left out, the movement can crack the wall. The note says that for most unrestrained brickwork the total movement can be estimated as the length of the brickwork multiplied by 0.0009. On a 25 ft (300 in.) run that is about 0.27 in. (our arithmetic from the note's figure), which is why the joint has to be soft enough to close up as the wall grows.

2. The spacing rules

  • No openings: no more than 25 ft (7.6 m) on center.
  • Multiple openings: consider symmetrical placement and reduced spacing of no more than 20 ft (6.1 m) on center.
  • Parapets: where joints are more than 15 ft (4.6 m) apart, make them wider or add a joint halfway between the full height joints.
  • Extend joints to the top of the brickwork, including parapets.

The note also gives a formula for the theoretical maximum spacing, Se = (wj x ej) / 0.09, where Se is the spacing in inches, wj is the joint width in inches and ej is the percent compressibility of the least compressible of the sealant, backer and filler. Its worked example, a 1/2 in. joint with a sealant that is 50 percent compressible, gives 278 in. or 23 ft 2 in. The table applies the same formula to other combinations (our arithmetic, rounded down to whole inches). Even when the formula allows more, the 25 ft and 20 ft limits still apply.

The note says compressibility of 25 to 50 percent is typical for modern sealants in brickwork, and that in most cases it is wise to be conservative. This is a design calculation for the engineer, not a field shortcut.

3. Where the joints go

Technical Note 18A lists the places where walls concentrate stress: corners, offsets and setbacks, wall intersections, changes in wall height, changes in backing or support, and changes in wall function or exposure. Two of those matter most on site.

  • Corners. Walls expand toward their ends, which stresses the corner. The preferred position is within 2 ft of the corner, and within about 10 ft of at least one side. The distances to the first joint on the two walls should not add up to more than the straight wall spacing, so with 25 ft spacing you could have 10 ft on one side and 15 ft on the other.
  • Openings. Windows and doors act like natural expansion joints, and cracks tend to start at the corners of punched openings. Joints are therefore often lined up with the edges of openings, where the layout allows it.

Expansion joints at inside corners are less visible, according to the note.

4. Build the joint so it works

  • Form the joint as the wall goes up, and keep mortar, ties and joint reinforcement from bridging it.
  • Use a premolded foam or neoprene pad through the full wythe thickness. Fiberboard is not compressible enough.
  • Size the joint to look like a mortar joint, usually 3/8 in. to 1/2 in.
  • Seal the outside with an ASTM C920, Grade NS, Use M sealant, Class 50 recommended and Class 25 as an alternate, over a circular backer rod about 25 percent larger than the joint, with sealant depth about half the joint width and at least 1/4 in. Many sealants need a primer, so test adhesion on the actual brick before the job.
  • Where a joint has to be cut into finished brickwork, align it so it does not leave thin slivers of brick beside the cut.

Joint finish and mortar choice are separate questions, covered in mortar joint profiles, mortar joint thickness and which mortar for brickwork.

5. Horizontal joints at shelf angles

Where the brick wythe sits on a shelf angle fixed to the frame, or fills in a frame, the note calls for a horizontal expansion joint under each angle: an unobstructed space, typically 1/4 in. (6 mm) high, or a highly compressible material, with a backer rod and sealant at the toe of the angle. Taller stories or an angle that carries more than one story can need a bigger joint. The International Masonry Institute adds that the joint should not contain mortar or shims that stop it moving. See also weep holes in brick walls for the flashing and drainage at these levels.

Brick expansion joint FAQ

How far apart should vertical expansion joints be in brick veneer?

BIA Technical Note 18A says to space vertical expansion joints no more than 25 ft (7.6 m) on center in brickwork without openings, and no more than 20 ft (6.1 m) for brickwork with multiple openings. It adds that no single recommendation fits every structure, so each wall should be analyzed for the movement expected. The International Masonry Institute gives the same rule of thumb of about 25 ft on center for veneer without openings.

Where do expansion joints go besides long straight walls?

Technical Note 18A says to place them at or near corners, at offsets and setbacks, at wall intersections, at changes in wall height, where the wall backing system changes, where support of the veneer changes, and where wall function or climate exposure changes. They should extend to the top of the brickwork, including parapets.

How close to a corner should the first joint be?

The note's preferred location is within 2 ft (600 mm) of the corner on either side, because a mason can usually reach about 2 ft around a corner. An expansion joint should be within about 10 ft (3 m) of at least one side of the corner. The two distances from the corner to the first joint in each wall should not add up to more than the straight wall spacing, for example 10 ft on one side and 15 ft on the other when the straight wall spacing is 25 ft.

How wide should an expansion joint be and what goes in it?

Technical Note 18A says the joint is typically sized to look like a mortar joint, usually 3/8 in. (10 mm) to 1/2 in. (13 mm). It is sealed on the outside with a sealant that complies with ASTM C920, Grade NS, Use M, with Class 50 recommended and Class 25 an alternate. Silicone is generally recommended on brickwork. A circular foam backer rod sits behind the sealant, sized about 25 percent larger than the joint width, and the sealant depth should be about half the joint width with a minimum of 1/4 in. (6.4 mm).

Can mortar or ties cross an expansion joint?

No. The note says to prevent mortar, ties or wire joint reinforcement from bridging the expansion joint, because that restricts movement and the joint will not work as intended. A premolded foam or neoprene pad through the full wythe thickness helps keep mortar and debris out. Fiberboard and similar materials are not suitable because they are not as compressible.

Do I need a horizontal expansion joint under a shelf angle?

Yes, where brick is supported on a shelf angle. Technical Note 18A says horizontal expansion joints are placed immediately below shelf angles, with a minimum 1/4 in. (6.4 mm) space or compressible material recommended, sealed with a backer rod and sealant at the toe of the angle. Taller story heights or an angle that supports more than one story of brick may need a larger joint, and the joint must not be filled with mortar or shims.

Is this the same as a control joint in a block wall?

No. The note says expansion joints, control joints, building expansion joints and construction joints each have a specific application and should not be used interchangeably. A control joint is for concrete or concrete masonry that shrinks, while an expansion joint is for clay brick that expands. The International Masonry Institute adds that when concrete brick, calcium silicate brick or stone are used, different movement joint requirements may apply.

Who decides where the joints go on a job?

The designer. The International Masonry Institute says building designers are responsible for showing movement joint type and location on the drawings, per TMS 402/602. Technical Note 18A says final decisions rest with the project architect, engineer and owner. A bricklayer should build the joints as drawn and raise any conflict, such as a joint that lands on a window, with the designer before laying.
